# Laws

These are the opinionated operating laws of the skill. They are intentionally sharper than the books themselves.

## Law 1 — Start with the read path

If you cannot say what a person sees first, second, and third, the design is not finished.

Apply:
- name the primary focal point
- name the secondary support
- reduce or demote everything else

Failure mode:
Everything has the same visual temperature.

## Law 2 — Build an armature before adding attitude

A page, screen, or surface needs a governing order before it needs personality.

Apply:
- establish grid, alignment logic, grouping, and rhythm first
- only then decide where to break pattern

Failure mode:
Expressive chaos pretending to be sophistication.

## Law 3 — One dominant move per surface

Every view gets one main idea:
- one hero image
- one typographic shock
- one major color event
- one structural surprise

Failure mode:
Three “hero” moves cancel one another out.

## Law 4 — White space must do real work

White space is not empty. It creates grouping, pause, emphasis, and status.

Apply:
- use it to isolate what matters
- use it to prevent adjacent elements from competing
- use it to make dense information feel intentional rather than cramped

Failure mode:
Padding everywhere, clarity nowhere.

## Law 5 — Typography is the voice, not the garnish

Type determines tone before the content is even read.

Apply:
- choose a typographic system that fits the product’s actual character
- prefer fewer families, stronger scale changes, and more disciplined weights
- make body type feel inevitable and easy, not merely acceptable

Failure mode:
Default SaaS typography with expensive adjectives attached to it.

## Law 6 — Use fewer fonts and more contrast

Do not solve weak hierarchy by collecting typefaces.

Apply:
- usually use one family with enough range, or two families with distinct jobs
- create hierarchy through size, weight, spacing, measure, case, and placement

Failure mode:
A “designed” look that reads like indecision.

## Law 7 — Make the small type worth reading

Taste collapses fastest in the details:
- captions
- table labels
- helper text
- nav items
- settings panels
- empty states
- chart notes

Apply:
- make secondary text clean, calm, and legible
- let microcopy sit in a confident rhythm

Failure mode:
A polished hero section sitting on top of cheap-looking internals.

## Law 8 — Color is relational, not absolute

Never choose color in isolation.

Apply:
- test color next to neutrals, text, borders, states, and data highlights
- judge accent colors by what they do to surrounding elements
- adjust quantity, not just hue

Failure mode:
A beautiful palette board producing muddy UI.

## Law 9 — Prefer tension to dead harmony

Pleasantness is not the goal. Conviction is.

Apply:
- allow a little dissonance
- let dark/light, warm/cool, big/small, dense/open produce energy
- avoid frictionless prettiness when the message needs edge

Failure mode:
Balanced, tasteful, forgettable.

## Law 10 — Quantity is quality

How much of a thing appears changes what it means.

Apply:
- use small areas of intense color
- let neutrals dominate when seriousness matters
- give emphasis through amount, not only saturation
- remember recurrence also counts as quantity

Failure mode:
Accent colors used so often they stop accenting.

## Law 11 — Shape density, don’t just add elements

Good surfaces have a rhythm of compactness and release.

Apply:
- alternate dense and open zones
- let large masses counter small details
- build beats, not soup

Failure mode:
Endless medium-sized components in a uniform grid.

## Law 12 — Imagery must earn its space

An image is not there because a rectangle looked empty.

Apply:
- use imagery to establish mood, evidence, scale, or structure
- crop harder
- let one strong image do the work of three weak ones

Failure mode:
Stock-photo reassurance pasted into a system that did not need it.

## Law 13 — Break the grid only after the grid is felt

Asymmetry without an invisible order is noise.

Apply:
- bleed, crop, offset, overlap, and diagonalize only when the underlying structure remains legible in the whole

Failure mode:
Random offsets sold as editorial energy.

## Law 14 — Navigation should be boring in the best way

The expressive parts can be expressive because navigation is dependable.

Apply:
- make wayfinding calm, stable, and unsurprising
- reserve visual drama for content, not for basic control structures

Failure mode:
A beautiful UI that makes ordinary tasks feel uncertain.

## Law 15 — Components should feel authored, not purchased

Buttons, cards, tables, forms, drawers, and charts should feel like they belong to one belief system.

Apply:
- align radius, stroke, shadow, density, spacing, type, motion, and icon logic
- remove accidental variety

Failure mode:
A brand voice on the landing page and a vendor UI everywhere else.

## Law 16 — Motion must clarify spatial change

Motion is for orientation, causality, and felt quality.

Apply:
- use movement to explain what changed, where it came from, and what is now active
- keep timings decisive
- prefer fewer better transitions

Failure mode:
Decorative motion that delays comprehension.

## Law 17 — Make responsive behavior part of the concept

Do not let mobile be a collapsed desktop.

Apply:
- decide what stays primary on the smallest screen
- recompose hierarchy, spacing, and grouping intentionally
- let the structure adapt without losing the brand’s character

Failure mode:
A good desktop design turned into a vertical content dump.

## Law 18 — Finish the edges

Taste often lives in the borders:
- baseline alignment
- icon optical balance
- table rulings
- chart labels
- hover states
- crop consistency
- page endings
- divider logic

Apply:
- do a final pass only for edge refinement

Failure mode:
Big ideas undermined by sloppy joins.

## Law 19 — Remove the generic

If the same result could have come from a random template in ten minutes, keep going.

Apply:
- look for places where authorship can appear through proportion, editing, rhythm, contrast, and specific judgment
- not every surface needs eccentricity, but every good surface needs intention

Failure mode:
“Looks nice” but belongs to no one.

## Law 20 — The system is the brand

A logo cannot rescue incoherent behavior.

Apply:
- ensure the same taste shows up in hierarchy, spacing, state design, image logic, naming, and interaction patterns
- judge the whole, not the badge

Failure mode:
Branding applied only as a skin.

## Law 21 — Style follows stance

Ask what the product believes.

Examples:
- serious/trustworthy → quieter neutrals, calmer spacing, firmer alignment
- youthful/energetic → sharper scale jumps, brighter accents, more asymmetry
- luxurious → restraint, spacing, material contrast, typographic confidence
- technical/expert → structure, data clarity, low-drama color, high signal density
- editorial/cultural → stronger image-text interplay, daring crops, asymmetrical tension

Failure mode:
The surface says “premium” while the interaction says “template.”

## Law 22 — Say no more often

Bad taste is often excess:
- one more weight
- one more border
- one more background tint
- one more chart color
- one more explanatory label

Apply:
When in doubt, remove before adding.

Failure mode:
Nothing is wrong individually; the accumulation is the problem.
